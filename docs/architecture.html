Videtor Architecture

A local tool that takes a folder of raw footage, consults a knowledge base built from your past Premiere Pro edits, and hands back a timeline that opens in Premiere Pro or DaVinci Resolve with the rough cut and sound bed already in place.

Owner Paritosh Scope MVP: knowledge base + first timeline Status knowledge base in two edit types · all 7 drives captured · first loop closed in Premiere · Round 3 done · Round 4 (cut from a brief) built and approved on Guru · standardised · ready for a blind test

What it does, in one pass

You point it at a folder. It indexes and analyzes every clip, reads your style profile, plans an edit, and writes a timeline file you import into your NLE. You finish the edit as you normally would. When you export that finished sequence back out, the tool compares it with what it proposed and updates the profile. The NLE stays the place where editing happens; the tool only prepares the timeline and learns from what you change.

$ videtor kb ingest ~/Exports/*.xml            # build the knowledge base from past edits
$ videtor ingest ~/Footage/2026-10-shoot       # index the raw folder (ffprobe, hashes, grouping)
$ videtor analyze                              # shots · transcript · silence · beats · loudness
$ videtor plan --type brand-film --brief "90s teaser" # that type's profile + brief → OTIO timeline
$ videtor export --to premiere                 # out/teaser.xml → File ▸ Import in Premiere
$ videtor kb learn ~/Exports/teaser-final.xml  # diff your finished cut vs. the proposal → profile

Architecture

Round 4 · designed 29 Oct

Cutting from a brief

You say what the piece is for before anything is cut. Claude turns that brief into a shot map: the sections of the piece and, for every slot, what the shot has to show. Videtor fills each slot with the best matching second of footage, ranked by your taste, and sends the cut to Premiere with two alternates per slot. When you fix the cut, Videtor reads your changes back over the bridge and the taste model learns from them. The pipeline above stays; the brief becomes its first input, and what you asked for replaces the planner’s guesses about what the piece needs.

The brief you write

Plain text in the project folder, five to ten lines. An example, not Guru’s real brief:

piece:  20 s teaser · 9:16 · Instagram
type:   brand-social · music: track.wav · speech: none
goal:   launch the new collection; intimate, premium
beats:
  hook     glimpses while she gets ready: makeup,
           hands, fabric; face only half seen
  reveal   her in the full outfit, camera easing out
  product  details on the beat: embroidery, jewellery
  story    candid moments, a laugh, a walk
  end      hero pose to camera
must:   one look to camera
avoid:  crew in frame, phone screens
like:   the pace of Vivo 2

How a slot gets filled

For each slot, Videtor searches the footage map for seconds whose picture matches the slot’s description (CLIP text-to-image search over the frames) and that pass its filters: shot scale, a face shown fully or in part, hands, a moving or still camera, speech. Every candidate gets one score from four parts: how well it matches; how its take compares with what you usually keep (sharpness, take length, resolution); how good the moment is within its take (focus on the subject, camera settled or mid-move, expression, distance from the start of the take); and what the taste model has learned from your fixes. Then all slots are filled together, so each take is used once and the strongest shots go where the brief needs them most. Each slot comes back with its pick, two alternates and the reasons.

The CLIP model in use (ViT-B/32) recognises hands, makeup, fabric, crowds and products, and is weaker on qualities such as confident or candid. The benchmark below measures what that costs; a larger local model or the second look are the fixes.

Why this fits what we measured

  • Your picks depend on the piece. Guru and Vivo 2 kept the talking clips and Vivo 1 avoided them; only Guru opens on a hook; a ranker trained across the nine edits without the brief barely beats the hand rules.
  • Inside a take, one frame does not show why you chose a second. In 182 takes you used, the frame from your moment is sharper than the take’s other sampled frames 48% of the time and has the bigger face 50% of the time, against 50% by chance. The slot says what the second must show, so the search can look for it.
  • You already shortlist moments. In the Guru project, 66 of the 157 takes carry in/out marks from the Source Monitor, every one of the 43 takes in the edit among them, and 23 marked takes never made the final cut. The XML export does not carry those marks; the bridge read all of them in under five seconds.
  • The current picker finds takes and misses seconds. Of the Guru teaser’s 24 shots, 14 come from takes you used and 16 from takes you marked, but only 2 land on a second you marked or used.

How we’ll know it works

The design is tested on past pieces before any new footage. You write the brief for Guru the way you would have before cutting it, without listing the shots you used; then Vivo 1 and Vivo 2, which share the drive. Each brief runs through map, pick and cut and is scored against your real edit and your marks: the right take, the right second (inside a range you used or marked), and whether your shot is among the three Videtor offers for its slot. Today’s picker on the Guru teaser: 58% right take, 8% right second.

Your call: frames to Claude

By default no frame leaves your Mac. Claude works from text, and the visual matching runs locally. The second look is opt-in per project: Claude sees small contact sheets of the top three picks per slot (about a hundred frames for a 20 s piece) and says which fits the brief best. It helps most where CLIP is weakest, and it sends client footage to Anthropic, so it stays off unless you switch it on for a project.

The knowledge base

Premiere's File ▸ Export ▸ Final Cut Pro XML produces an xmeml file that contains far more than cut points: every clip's in/out and source, the track it sits on, transitions, audio level keyframes, speed changes, and the names of effects applied. Parsed into OpenTimelineIO, that is enough to compute a style profile without ever opening Premiere. The table lists what the miner extracts in Phase 1 and what the planner does with each signal.

Because you cut everything from interviews to sports promos, there is no single "your style". The profile is keyed by edit type: kb/profile/talking-head.json, kb/profile/brand-film.json, kb/profile/sports-promo.json, and so on. The miner assigns each past sequence a type from its own evidence (speech-to-music ratio, median shot length, share of cuts on the beat) and you correct it in a one-line manifest. The brief names the type for a new job, or the tool infers it from the footage and asks.

What the XML alone cannot teach

An export records what you kept. Learning what you discard (the weak takes, the flubbed lines) needs the raw footage alongside the XML. You have that for three or more past projects, so take-selection learning is in Phase 1: the miner analyzes the unused material the same way as the used, and the profile records what separates them (speech present, motion, sharpness, duration). The remaining projects can be XML-only.

The feedback loop closes the same gap going forward: every finished edit you re-export is diffed against the proposal (clips you dropped, cuts you moved, levels you changed), and the deltas update the profile.

What the two profiles say

  • brand-social (7 sequences): vertical 1080×1920 at 25 fps, about 66 s long. Median shot 0.88 s (p10 0.25, p90 2.4), 64 cuts a minute, 31% of shots speed-changed, 20 adjustment layers a minute carrying “Flash blur” and “Flash 2”, Distort (and the odd Luma Key) as clip effects, Low Pass Filter on audio. First title at about 8 s.
  • sports-promo (2 sequences): vertical at 23.976 fps, about 2¼ min long. Median shot 1.21 s, 35 cuts a minute, speed changes on 7% of shots, 12 adjustment layers a minute (Flash blur only), Gaussian Blur as the clip effect, footage stacked 1.5 layers deep at the median.
  • Common to both: no video transitions to speak of (0–0.6% of edits), audio crossfaded on 38–44% of clips at 24–25 frames, B-roll inserts about 1 s long, 10–11 video and 14–18 audio tracks.
  • Most audio clips are left at unity. Where a level is touched: music beds about −10 dB, SFX about −6 dB, camera audio pushed +3 dB (brand) to +6.5 dB (sports).

What the miner still misses

  • Nested sequences are now opened up everywhere (pacing metrics and footage index): Guru’s 22 nests turn 54 shots into 88, Coca-Cola’s 22 turn 106 into 130, and the brand-social speed-change share rises from 17% to 31% because the ramped clips sit inside nests.
  • First footage index (Paritosh_1): Vivo 1 used 100 of 460 files in its folder and kept 25% of the duration of the clips it touched (2.5 h left unused); Vivo 2 used 66 of 340 and kept 11% (1.7 h unused); Guru used 43 of 157 and kept 5%. Gujarat Giants (sports-promo, AMAZONSSD) used 17 of 466 and kept 17% of the clips it touched, leaving a whole behind-the-scenes camera (117 clips) and the second camera (39 clips) out entirely. Zomato (AJAN SSD4) used 66 of 2,170 files and kept 1.4% of the 3.3 h of clips it touched, the most selective edit so far. JW (Extreme SSD) used 47 of 2,568 and kept 3.8%, drawing only on Day 2 of a two-day shoot. Coca-Cola (Paritosh_4) used 48 of 877 and kept 5.3% of the 31 min of clips it touched. That unused material is the training signal for take selection.
  • First used-vs-unused comparison from the sampled frames (videtor kb selects, 28 Oct): in all eight indexed edits the clips you used are sharper than the ones you left out (Laplacian variance 1.2× to 2.4× higher; Guru 89 vs 38, Vivo 1 53 vs 22, Adidas 106 vs 52), and in seven of eight they are longer takes (1.3× to 6.5×; Adidas picks 53 s takes from a pool whose median is 8 s). Brightness and contrast barely differ, so exposure is not how you choose. Frame rate shows a habit: 50 and 100 fps slow-motion material is mostly left unused in Vivo 2, Gujarat Giants and JW. These are the first candidate rules for take selection; motion, speech and shot scale come next.
  • Audio roles (camera, bed, SFX) are structural guesses until the analysis stage can listen.
  • Where cuts land relative to the beat needs the music files themselves; the exports point at the /Volumes/Paritosh_3 drive.
  • No LUT or input-color metadata is present in these exports; log handling will come from the footage.

Recommended stack

Dependencies we install

  • Python 3.11 via uv. The Mac's system Python is 3.9, and Resolve's scripting module needs 3.10/3.11 if we ever go that route.
  • ffmpeg / ffprobe via Homebrew (not installed yet).
  • opentimelineio + otio-fcp-adapter for the timeline model and xmeml in/out.
  • scenedetect, faster-whisper (or mlx-whisper on Apple Silicon), librosa, pyloudnorm.
  • pydantic for the profile and EDL schemas, typer for the CLI.
  • No network calls in Phase 1. The planner sits behind one interface so a local model (via Ollama or MLX) or the anthropic SDK can be added in Phase 2 once you decide to explore API integration.

Designs we borrow, code we write

  • Transcript packing and the EDL-as-intermediate from video-use.
  • Rank-then-select-to-duration planning from RankCut.
  • Saved, re-applicable editing recipes from OpenStoryline's Skills, as our kb/notes.md rules.
  • Label-to-action edit model and NLE XML quirks from auto-editor.
  • Everything is plain files in the repo: kb/, cache/, out/. No database until there is a reason.

Phase 1Knowledge base + first timeline (MVP)
~1–2 weeks
  • kb ingest: parse every XML, assign each sequence an edit type, compute the signals in the table above, write kb/profile/<type>.json. For the projects with raw footage, analyze the unused clips too and record what separates kept from discarded.
  • ingest + analyze: media index; shot boundaries, speech presence on every clip and word-level transcripts only where speech is found (decided 28 Oct: interviews and voxpops matter, most footage is music-led), silence, loudness, motion energy per shot (for sports and fast-paced work), shot scale, beats of any supplied music; results cached per clip. Built 28 Oct: videtor analyze <drive> runs three feature modules over a footage index and caches one JSON per clip in cache/analysis/: looks from the sampled frames (sharpness, exposure, colorfulness), audio (EBU R128 loudness, silence spans, speech presence by WebRTC VAD with a syllabic-modulation check so tones and music do not count as speech), and motion (frame-difference energy and in-clip cut detection from low-res hardware-decoded frames). Resumable; frame features run without the drive. Still to come: transcription of speech clips, shot scale, music beats.
  • sync: audio cross-correlation aligns multicam angles and external-recorder audio to picture; clips covering the same moment are grouped so the planner chooses angles, not just clips. Log footage is normalized to Rec.709 before frame analysis.
  • plan v0, rules only, parameterized by the edit type's profile: order clips by timecode or name, drop silence and dead air, snap cuts to shot boundaries, pace to the profile's median shot length, A-roll on V1, music on A2 at the profile's level with gain keyframes ducking under speech.
  • export --to premiere | otio: built 28 Oct. videtor plan picks, cuts and orders shots from a footage pool (sharpness, take length, resolution, frame rate, motion; shot lengths drawn from the edit type’s own distribution; in-points from your habit), writes plan.json with a reason per shot plus the Premiere XML; videtor kb benchmark scores a plan against the real edit on clips, moments and pace.
Benchmark (decided 28 Oct): the first run re-cuts the Guru project from its raw folder on Paritosh_1 (157 clips, 27 min) and is scored against the real Guru edit: which clips it picked, where it cut, how the pacing compares. v0 baseline, 28 Oct, frame features only: 35 shots, 44.9 s; 20 of the 35 clips are ones you used (precision 57%, recall 47%, chance ~27%); under 1 s of planned footage falls inside the moments you used, so in-point choice is the first thing to learn; planned median shot 0.92 s against your 0.62 s. The footage indexes give one habit: across 564 used ranges in nine edits your in-point sits a median 38% into the take, a third within the first quarter and 15% in the last (an earlier reading of 23% came from a rate bug, below). The planner samples in-points from that distribution and keeps a slot inside one uncut run; clip precision holds at 54–57% over three seeds, while the moments score stays near 1–2%: which half-second of a 12 s take you pick will need content cues (motion peaks, faces, speech), the Phase 2 planner’s job. v1 (29 Oct), with audio and motion analysed on the drive: in Guru 70% of the clips you kept contain speech against 48% of the rest, and kept clips move slightly more; the planner now rewards speech and motion and cuts into speech clips a breath before the longest phrase. Clip precision rises to 54–63% (21 of 35 at seed 0), recall stays at 44–49%. Rate convention, found 29 Oct when the first imported cut showed black frames: Premiere counts a clipitem’s in/out at the clipitem’s <rate>, which is the sequence rate even for 50 or 100 fps media (only the <file> block carries the native rate). The writer had counted at the media rate, so 100 fps slots ran off the end of their files; the reader had the same bug, so used ranges for high-frame-rate clips were up to four times too early in 264 of the 406 used clips, and the moments score was measured against the wrong ranges. Both are fixed and every drive recomputed offline. Verdict on the rebuilt v1 in Premiere (29 Oct): every shot plays with picture; as a first assembly you would keep about a quarter to a half of the 35 shots. Your direction for the next planner: hook first, then product shots carried along with the story, cuts on the beat of the music; shoot order is not the structure of this kind of piece. v2 and the 20 s fast cut (29 Oct): librosa beat tracking on a stand-in song (Guru’s own track is gone from Downloads), strongest shot first, every cut on the grid, music on A2. Your verdict on v2 was “cuts feel off the beat”, and the one edit whose music is still reachable explains why: your 10 Vivo 2 cuts under that song land a median 0.14 s before the detected beat and never on it, so the planner now cuts 120 ms ahead of the beat, frame-exact from the beat time so rounding cannot drift. What the first loop showed: clip selection works on stats (sharpness, take length, resolution, speech per brief), pacing and beat-cutting work, and three things do not: which second of a take, which shot opens, and product vs story. Your definition of the hook sets the target: a tease of two or three shots in which the subject is only partly revealed (makeup being done, a half-visible figure, some blur allowed, close-ups of attire, apparel and hands), then the zoom-out that reveals the main subject.

Decisions and open questions

Decided 28 Oct

  1. Content: everything from talking-head and vlogs to events, brand films, ads and fast-paced sports edits. Consequence: the knowledge base is keyed by edit type, and every planner rule takes the type's profile as its parameters.
  2. Raw footage is still on disk for three or more past projects. Consequence: learning take selection (what you discard) is in Phase 1, not deferred.
  3. Premiere Pro first. Export is tuned and verified against Premiere; Resolve import is checked at each milestone but not tuned until Phase 2.
  4. Local only to start. Phase 1 makes no network calls. The planner is one interface with a rule-based backend; a local model or the Claude API slots in behind it when you decide to explore API integration.
  1. Footage: single camera and multicam, often several points of view shot on different cameras at different locations and focal lengths; log footage; a separate audio recorder.   2. Music and SFX: one licensed track per project, plus a music library and an SFX library on disk.
